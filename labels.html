Where these numbers came from

They are estimates, and they are not label-legal. Nothing here has been pressed, pasteurized or sent to a lab. Before a single can is printed, every figure has to be replaced with results from an accredited food-testing laboratory — that is a legal requirement, not a formality.

What these are is a defensible model: good enough to formulate against, price against, and show a co-packer.

Sugar is computed straight from the vineyard's own Brix board. Degrees Brix is very close to grams of sugar per 100 mL in grape juice, so sugar = 355 mL × juice% × Brix/100 is a sound first estimate.

Why the juice percentages look the way they do. Ripe wine grapes here run 22–25 Brix — gloriously sweet. To land a light, crisp can we pick earlier fruit: at 17–19 Brix the same variety brings all its flavor with more acid and less sugar, so every can stays in the 9–13g range without diluting the taste away.

Micronutrients use USDA reference values for grape juice, scaled to the juice actually in the can. Grape juice is a genuinely useful source of potassium, and that carries straight through to the panel — the numbers printed here are simply what the grapes bring.

Protein, fat and fiber round to zero at these juice volumes. That's honest, and it's also why this is a drink, not a food.
